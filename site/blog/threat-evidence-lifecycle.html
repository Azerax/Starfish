Threats as governed evidence, not a signature list

v0.26.0 introduces the Threat Immunity Fabric: a detection stops being a flat rule you either trust or don't, and becomes an evidence object with a lifecycle, a quorum, dual control, a seal, and a place on the hash chain. Four of ten increments shipped. The most important property is what it structurally cannot do.

Every threat-intelligence system has the same shape: something notices an attack, and that observation becomes a rule everyone runs. The part that usually goes unexamined is the middle — how an observation earns the authority to change what a system refuses. In most products the answer is "a vendor said so," and the rule arrives as a signature you either trust wholesale or disable wholesale.

Starfish already applies a governed lifecycle to memory writes and tool calls: proposer ≠ approver, quorum where stakes are high, content-bound approvals, everything audited. Threat detections were the one thing still handled ad hoc. This release fixes that asymmetry.

The honest framing from the design plan up front: most of the architecture already existed. Of eighteen proposed sections, seven needed no new architecture at all — just wiring and naming over gateways already shipped — seven were extensions of existing modules, and four were genuinely new. What was actually missing was a governed evidence lifecycle to carry a detection from "something looked wrong" to "adopted rule."

The lifecycle

Eight stages, with the legal transitions expressed as an explicit frozen edge table rather than inferred from ordering:

observed     → reproduced | revoked
reproduced   → corroborated | revoked
corroborated → approved | revoked
approved     → published | revoked
published    → deployed | retired | revoked
deployed     → retired | revoked
retired      → (terminal)
revoked      → (terminal)

Three things in that table are deliberate.

revoked is reachable from every non-terminal stage. Revocation must be faster than the ordinary path and must never be gated behind reaching a particular stage first. If you have to promote a bad rule through validation before you can withdraw it, your withdrawal mechanism is theatre.

published is the only stage with three outbound edges. Deployment is optional — published evidence can retire without ever being deployed anywhere.

Terminal means terminal, not deleted. A revoked or retired record stays in the ledger; it simply cannot move again. Nothing is ever hard-removed.

What each stage actually demands

Observe mints the envelope and seals it. Critically, this is not an authorization — containment happens before this call, by the caller. Observing only records what was seen and what was already contained.

Reproduce accepts a {ok, detail} verdict from whoever ran the replay. The module never runs anything itself, and a failed reproduction does not advance the stage — it audits a denial and leaves the record where it was. The project's "no unbacked word" principle applies to evidence exactly as it applies to an agent's claims.

Corroborate requires distinct validator identities, and the publisher cannot be one of them. Attempting it audits a denial and throws. The quorum default is two, on the same reasoning the confidence engine uses: corroboration from a single validator is not corroboration. Submissions are idempotent, so the same validator calling twice does not manufacture a quorum.

Approve enforces two separate rules that are easy to conflate: the approver must not be the publisher, and the approver must be in the approver set. A validator becoming the approver is fine. What must never happen is a publisher approving its own evidence.

Publish marks a record ready for a broker to distribute. It performs no network I/O — the governance core stays network-free, the same discipline the external-source gateway holds.

Deploy records that a receiving organization turned a recommendation into a running local action. It records the fact; it does not perform it. More on that below, because it is the crux.

Refusals are audited before the throw. Publisher self-corroboration, publisher self-approval, approval by a non-approver, and failed reproduction all leave a decision: 'deny' entry on the chain. The ledger records attempted violations, not just successful transitions — which is the difference between an audit trail and a changelog.

The seal, and the one subtlety in it

Each envelope carries a sha256 seal over its observe-time facts, re-verified on every lookup — so the seal is checked before any transition, revoke or expire, not merely on demand. A mismatch means an out-of-band edit rather than a lifecycle step.

The subtlety is what is deliberately excluded from the seal. reproductionCaseRef is by definition unknown until reproduce() runs, so sealing it at observe time would make every successful reproduction look like tampering on the very next transition. The validation block is excluded for the same reason — the lifecycle methods only ever write there. What stays sealed is what the observation actually fixed: the content commitment and the decision receipt.

That content commitment is a hash, not content. The raw candidate material never travels in the envelope. You can share an indicator of a prompt-injection payload without shipping the payload.

Indicators come in six channels — exact hashes, normalized fragments, fuzzy fingerprints, semantic intents, structural features, behavioural indicators — so a detection can be shared at whatever fidelity is defensible rather than being forced into a single exact-match form.

The property that matters most is a negative one

A threat fabric is an obvious route to a second decision engine. High confidence that something is malicious feels like it should authorize a response. The plan refused that from the start, and the refusal is structural rather than a promise:

No envelope, however high its confidence or however many validators signed it, calls the policy decision point. It can only add weight to a risk category the existing scorer already reads.

Concretely: FabricAction — observe | warn | restrict | quarantine | block-local | block-tenant | block-domain — is advisory input. Nothing in this release calls pdp.decide(). The types module imports type-only and has zero logic. And buildDecisionReceipt can only wrap a RiskAssessment the one scorer already produced — it copies a score in, it cannot originate one. There is no code path by which the fabric computes risk.

Severity reuses the existing risk vocabulary rather than inventing a second scale, with one exclusion that shows the care taken: injection is excluded from threat severity, because in the scorer that value means "off-scale hard reject," not a graded severity a validator assigns.

Two detector classes are deliberately unbuilt for the same reason. Semantic detection needs a model call, and the core stays network-free and off the PDP's synchronous path — the 10ms gate target is treated as a floor, not a target to negotiate away. Canary detection depends on a deception cell that is still design-only.

Four of ten, and which four

Shipped: the envelope and decision-receipt schema; structural and behavioural detector inputs; the lifecycle state machine; and ledger wiring — every transition audited under a new threat domain, which was the only vocabulary addition needed, because the hash chain and optional external anchoring already existed.

Not built: the reproduction sandbox; role-scoped trust-fabric identity; the cross-organization broker; the local adoption engine; progressive deployment staging; anti-poisoning and publisher reputation; and the governed detector-learning loop.

The gap worth naming is the local adoption engine. It is the only increment that would let a threat recommendation change a decision — and even then by adding category evidence to the single scorer's input, never by ruling on a call. Today deploy() records that adoption happened while no mechanism performs it. It sits two increments deep behind identity and the broker, both of which are blocked on real decisions: how long a key-rotation epoch should be, and whether the threat-feed manifest lives in this GitHub org or fully outside it.

Everything through the shipped four runs fully local, with zero cross-organization sharing, and is independently useful. The later increments are what would turn it into a fabric.

The sequencing deviation, stated rather than buried

The lifecycle shipped without the reproduction sandbox that the plan's own build order put first. That was a deliberate scope call, not an oversight: reproduce() is a pure state transition accepting a result from whoever calls it, so today that caller has to be a human validator or an external process. The sandbox is the prerequisite for automating reproduction — not for the lifecycle gate itself, which is why the gate could ship ahead of it.

The alternative was scored and rejected. Dropping the reproduced stage entirely scored lowest of five options, for a specific reason: it violates the project's own "no unbacked word" principle, because corroboration is not reproduction. Two people agreeing they saw something is not the same as the thing happening again.

When the sandbox does land it composes existing modules rather than adding primitives — egress denial from the network guard, scratch-directory-only filesystem from the boundary engine, capture-not-apply from containment. A disposable no-egress context that captures a candidate's proposed effects rather than letting them happen.

One more non-goal worth repeating, because it is the same residual as everywhere else: no hardware-backed signing keys this pass. Software Ed25519, with TPM/HSM treated as a later hardening item in the same tier as OS-level sandboxing. The honest ceiling on all of this is still that Starfish governs a cooperative process and does not contain a hostile one.
